NFL Race, Labor, and Data Visualization

Racial Stacking, Bodily Risk, and Institutional Power in the NFL

This project examines how racialized positional segregation in football shaped who was trusted to lead, who was expected to absorb physical risk, and how those patterns continue to appear in modern NFL data.

Leif Blake
Introduction & Methodology

Race, Labor, and the Structure of Football

American football has long been presented as a meritocratic institution in which athletes succeed through talent, discipline, and physical performance alone. However, sociologists studying race and sport have consistently demonstrated that football positions were historically shaped through racialized assumptions regarding intelligence, leadership, athleticism, and bodily labor. This project examines how “racial stacking” within the National Football League (NFL) created unequal distributions of positional authority, bodily risk, and institutional power. Specifically, this project argues that Black athletes were disproportionately funneled into positions associated with physical sacrifice and bodily exposure, while positions associated with strategic leadership and organizational authority historically remained disproportionately white.

To examine these patterns, this project combines historical scholarship, sociological analysis, computational methods, and data visualization. Rather than relying solely upon qualitative interpretation, the project integrates demographic datasets, injury reports, roster data, positional classifications, scouting language studies, and custom-built visualizations in order to examine how race, positional assignment, bodily labor, and institutional authority intersect throughout modern football culture.

Methodology

The primary demographic datasets used throughout the project originated from several publicly available football analytics repositories and independently cleaned datasets. Modern roster and positional information was obtained through nflverse, an open-source football analytics repository providing detailed weekly NFL roster data. Injury information was drawn from publicly available NFL injury datasets spanning multiple seasons between 2009 and 2017. Additional demographic summaries were developed through custom player metadata collection processes.

One of the largest methodological challenges involved constructing a large-scale racial demographic dataset for NFL players. Early stages of the project attempted to build an independent demographic database through automated scraping of player metadata from publicly available sources such as Wikipedia and Pro Football Reference. Python scripts were developed to export player templates, scrape metadata, and collect demographic information for thousands of active and historical NFL players. For example, scripts such as 03_export_race_template.py, 04_auto_wikipedia_metadata.py, and 05_create_race_data.py were used to automate portions of the data collection process.

However, the number of player entries quickly became too large to verify manually at scale. Because manually identifying and entering demographic information for thousands of players introduced major limitations involving time, verification accuracy, and consistency, the project shifted toward combining publicly available datasets with machine-assisted demographic classification methods.

Several publicly available GitHub projects and football analytics repositories incorporated DeepFace facial recognition tools and computer vision methods to estimate demographic classifications from player headshots and metadata. While these approaches introduced limitations and ethical considerations regarding racial classification, they provided a scalable foundation for examining large-scale positional demographics across the NFL. Rather than treating these classifications as definitive biological categories, this project uses them as broad sociological demographic groupings intended to analyze structural representation patterns across football positions.

Once the datasets were collected, significant preprocessing and cleaning operations were required to standardize positional categories across sources. Player positions were grouped into broader football labor categories such as quarterback (QB), running back (RB), wide receiver (WR), offensive line (OL), linebacker (LB), defensive line (DL), defensive back (DB), and tight end (TE). Earlier versions of the injury dataset grouped all defensive players into a single “DEF” category, which obscured meaningful positional variation across defensive labor. To improve positional accuracy, nflverse roster data was merged into the injury dataset using Python and pandas operations. 

players["injury_position_group"] = players["position_id"]

merged = injuries.merge(
    players[["name", "team", "year", "injury_position_group"]],
    on=["name", "team", "year"],
    how="left"
)

This merge allowed defensive positions to be separated into DB, LB, and DL categories rather than treating all defensive players as a single labor group. Additional aggregation functions calculated average injury burden, cumulative games missed, player-season totals, and racial representation percentages by position group. Injury burden itself was calculated using logic such as: 

merged["injury_burden"] = (
    merged["num_games_injured"] +
    merged["num_games_missing"]
)

The project also relied heavily upon custom D3.js and Python-based data visualization techniques. Sankey diagrams, heatmaps, stacked demographic charts, bubble charts, timeline visualizations, and scouting language charts were developed to visualize relationships between race, positional assignment, bodily risk, organizational hierarchy, and leadership stereotypes. Rather than functioning simply as aesthetic supplements, these visualizations serve as analytical tools that reveal structural relationships often obscured within traditional football discourse.

The visualization above provides a baseline overview of racial representation across major NFL positional groups using the cleaned 2024 NFL depth chart dataset developed for this project. Running back, wide receiver, defensive back, and defensive line positions remain heavily concentrated with Black athletes, while quarterback, offensive line, and tight end positions remain substantially whiter by comparison. These demographic patterns establish the empirical foundation for the analyses developed throughout the remainder of this project and demonstrate why positional assignment remains central to understanding racial inequality within football culture.

Combining historical scholarship with computational analysis allows this project to examine racial stacking not simply as a historical artifact, but as an ongoing institutional structure connecting race, bodily labor, leadership, organizational authority, and physical risk within the modern NFL. The following sections build upon this methodological foundation by tracing the historical origins of racial stacking, examining how institutional systems reinforced positional inequality, analyzing the unequal distribution of bodily risk, and exploring how media narratives and cultural discourse continue reproducing these structures today.

Section 1

Historical Foundations of Racial Stacking

For much of American football’s history, positions were not distributed neutrally. Instead, racial stereotypes shaped which athletes were trusted with leadership, intelligence, and strategic decision-making responsibilities and which athletes were valued primarily for bodily labor and physical performance. Sociologists refer to this process as “racial stacking,” or the disproportionate concentration of racial groups into specific positions based on social assumptions rather than ability alone. Within football, these patterns created a racial divide between positions associated with intelligence and authority and positions associated with speed, athleticism, and physical sacrifice.

The quarterback position became one of the clearest examples of this division. Historically, quarterbacks were viewed as extensions of coaching authority on the field, requiring discipline, leadership, intelligence, emotional composure, and strategic understanding of the game. Because of this symbolic importance, white athletes were disproportionately funneled into quarterback roles while Black athletes were systematically excluded. As Against Racial Stacking: The Evolution of the Black Quarterback in American Football explains, Black athletes were often “shifted away from quarterback and into positions emphasizing speed and athleticism” (“Against Racial Stacking,” 2024). The article further argues that football institutions historically framed quarterbacks as “thinking” players while Black athletes were stereotyped as naturally athletic rather than intellectually capable of leadership (“Against Racial Stacking,” 2024). These assumptions reinforced the belief that Black athletes were better suited for physically demanding positions such as running back, wide receiver, or defensive back.

The exclusion of Black athletes from quarterback positions reflected broader racial ideologies present throughout American society during the twentieth century. White athletes were often associated with discipline, intelligence, leadership, and rationality, while Black athletes were framed as instinctive, emotional, and naturally physical. These stereotypes allowed football institutions to justify racial segregation within positional systems while presenting those outcomes as meritocratic or “natural.” Rather than openly prohibiting Black quarterbacks, many coaches and scouts redirected Black athletes into positions believed to better align with racial assumptions about physicality and athleticism.

These barriers remained significant even as Black athletes increasingly dominated football participation overall. Black quarterbacks frequently faced pressure to switch positions at both the collegiate and professional levels. Players who demonstrated elite passing ability were still commonly converted into wide receivers, defensive backs, or running backs because coaches believed Black athletes lacked the intellectual qualities supposedly necessary to lead offenses. The Guardian notes that Black quarterbacks were routinely described as “athletic” or “dual-threat,” while white quarterbacks were praised for “preparation, intelligence and football IQ” (Dator, 2019). Even when Black quarterbacks succeeded, media narratives frequently framed their accomplishments through improvisation and raw talent rather than preparation or strategic understanding of the game.

The timeline above demonstrates how slowly these barriers began to shift. Milestones involving Black quarterbacks and coaches often represented moments of institutional resistance rather than simple athletic progress. The relatively late emergence of Black quarterbacks in leadership roles illustrates how deeply entrenched racial assumptions remained within football culture. Quarterback was never treated as merely another athletic position; it became a symbolic site where ideas about race, intelligence, leadership, and authority were constantly negotiated.

These racial assumptions extended beyond the quarterback position itself. Sociologists studying race and sport have argued that football developed a hierarchy of “thinking” and “physical” positions that mirrored broader social hierarchies. Positions such as quarterback, center, middle linebacker, and coaching roles were associated with leadership and strategic authority, while positions such as running back, wide receiver, and defensive back became associated with speed, aggression, and bodily sacrifice. The sociology review article on race and sport explains that athletic institutions historically celebrated Black athletes for physical performance while simultaneously limiting access to positions associated with organizational control and intellectual authority (Coakley, 2012). In this framework, Black athletes were welcomed into football primarily as performers of physical labor rather than as decision-makers or institutional leaders.

These assumptions were reinforced institutionally through scouting systems, draft interviews, and recruitment pipelines. Drew Brown’s Race, Colonization and the NFL Draft: A Fanonian Analysis of the Interviewing of Black NFL Prospects argues that NFL scouting and interviewing practices reproduced colonial stereotypes about Black physicality and white intellectual authority. Brown explains that Black prospects were frequently evaluated through assumptions emphasizing “raw athleticism” and bodily potential while white prospects were associated with leadership, discipline, and intelligence (Brown, 2023). These evaluations shaped not only draft outcomes but also the positions Black athletes were encouraged—or discouraged—from pursuing throughout football development systems.

Brown’s analysis is particularly important because it frames football not simply as entertainment, but as a racialized labor structure. Within this structure, Black athletes became disproportionately concentrated in positions involving bodily exposure, physical punishment, and athletic expendability, while white athletes retained disproportionate access to positions associated with organizational authority and long-term institutional power. These positional divisions reflected broader patterns of racial labor stratification throughout American society, where Black workers historically performed physically dangerous labor while white workers occupied supervisory or managerial positions.

Although overt racial exclusion became less socially acceptable during the late twentieth century, many of these positional patterns remained structurally embedded within football development systems. Youth coaching, recruiting pipelines, scouting language, and media narratives continued reproducing assumptions about which athletes were “natural leaders” and which athletes were “natural athletes.” As a result, historical patterns of racial stacking persisted even as the NFL became increasingly racially diverse overall.

The positional demographics shown above demonstrate how these historical structures continue to shape the modern NFL. Using a contemporary NFL depth chart dataset containing approximately 2,700 active players from the 2024 season, the Sankey diagram visualizes the racial distribution of players across major positional groups. Running back, wide receiver, and defensive back positions remain heavily concentrated with Black athletes, while quarterback and several offensive line positions remain disproportionately white. Although the league now features far more Black quarterbacks than in previous decades, the broader positional distribution still reflects historical assumptions about intelligence, authority, discipline, and bodily labor. The modern NFL therefore continues to reproduce many of the same positional patterns established during earlier periods of explicit racial exclusion.

Importantly, these patterns are not random outcomes of athletic preference alone. Rather, they reflect decades of institutional decision-making, cultural stereotypes, and developmental systems that sorted athletes according to racialized assumptions about leadership and physicality. Historical racial stacking therefore established more than positional segregation—it created a racial hierarchy of football labor itself. White athletes were historically associated with strategic authority and organizational leadership, while Black athletes were disproportionately directed toward positions centered on bodily performance and physical risk.

This historical distinction between intellectual authority and bodily labor laid the foundation for the structural inequalities explored throughout the remainder of this project. Modern positional demographics, injury burden disparities, and leadership hierarchies within football cannot be understood separately from the historical systems that shaped who was trusted to lead and who was expected to absorb physical risk on the field. While these patterns originated through overt racial segregation and exclusion, they continued to persist through institutional practices embedded within scouting systems, coaching pipelines, media narratives, and organizational decision-making. Understanding how these structures evolved from historical prejudice into modern institutional systems is essential for examining how racial inequality continues to operate throughout contemporary football culture.

Section 2

Institutional and Structural Discrimination

Although overt racial segregation within football declined throughout the late twentieth century, many of the systems that reinforced racial inequality remained embedded within the institutional structures surrounding the sport. Rather than operating primarily through explicit exclusion, modern football organizations increasingly reproduced inequality through hiring systems, organizational hierarchy, scouting infrastructure, and forms of unconscious racial bias. As a result, racial disparities persisted not simply because of individual prejudice, but because institutions continued rewarding and reproducing particular ideas about leadership, intelligence, and authority.

One of the clearest examples of this institutional inequality emerged through coaching and front office hiring practices. Despite the NFL becoming overwhelmingly Black at the player level, leadership positions within organizations remained disproportionately white. In The NFL’s Rooney Rule and Its Implications on Affirmative Action and Hiring Policies in Professional Sports, Cunningham and Melton (2011) argue that NFL ownership and management structures historically evaluated leadership through racialized assumptions about intelligence, discipline, and organizational competence. The authors explain that many owners and executives unconsciously associated white candidates with strategic thinking and leadership while Black candidates were frequently perceived as less capable of managing the “complexity” of football organizations (Cunningham & Melton, 2011). These assumptions helped institutionalize racial hierarchy within football organizations by shaping who received access to coaching, executive, and decision-making opportunities.

Importantly, these inequalities were often maintained through organizational systems that appeared neutral on the surface. Coaching and executive hires frequently relied on informal professional networks, ownership relationships, and long-standing organizational familiarity that overwhelmingly favored white candidates. Even after the implementation of the Rooney Rule, critics argued that minority interviews sometimes functioned symbolically rather than substantively. While the policy increased the visibility of Black coaching candidates, it did not fundamentally restructure the institutional networks controlling hiring decisions. As a result, leadership opportunities remained disproportionately concentrated among white candidates despite the league’s increasingly diverse player population.

Institutional systems also shaped how athletes were evaluated, recruited, and developed long before reaching professional football. In Race, Colonization and the NFL Draft: A Fanonian Analysis of the Interviewing of Black NFL Prospects, Drew Brown (2023) argues that scouting and interview practices frequently categorized athletes through racialized assumptions about leadership potential, composure, and discipline. These evaluation systems influenced not only draft outcomes, but also broader developmental pipelines throughout football culture. Because scouting reports, coaching decisions, and recruitment systems strongly influenced positional assignment, institutional bias became embedded directly into how athletes were developed within the sport.

Rather than operating through explicit racial exclusion, modern football organizations often reproduced inequality through coded language and standardized evaluation practices. Traits such as “leadership,” “coachability,” “football IQ,” and “professionalism” frequently carried implicit racial assumptions that shaped how athletes and coaching candidates were perceived within organizational settings. These systems allowed racial inequality to persist institutionally even as overt forms of discrimination became less publicly acceptable.

The visualizations above demonstrate how these institutional patterns continue shaping modern positional structures within the NFL. Using a 2024 NFL depth chart dataset containing approximately 2,700 active players, the charts compare racial concentration across positions associated with varying levels of symbolic authority and bodily risk. Positions such as quarterback remain disproportionately white and cluster more closely around positions associated with strategic authority, while positions such as running back, wide receiver, and defensive back remain heavily concentrated with Black athletes and align more closely with positions associated with physical exposure and bodily labor. The comparison between Black player distribution and white player distribution illustrates how organizational systems continue reproducing racialized positional hierarchies within the modern NFL.

The heatmap further demonstrates the persistence of these demographic patterns across major NFL positional groups using the same 2024 player dataset. Although the NFL frequently presents itself as a meritocratic institution in which athletes succeed purely through talent and effort, the league’s positional demographics continue reflecting longstanding organizational patterns surrounding leadership, authority, and positional assignment. While some diversity has increased in positions such as quarterback, broader racial concentration patterns remain remarkably consistent with the institutional structures established during earlier periods of racial exclusion.

These institutional systems carried consequences extending beyond representation alone. By disproportionately concentrating Black athletes within positions associated with physical exposure and bodily sacrifice, organizational structures also shaped how bodily risk and injury burden became distributed throughout football. Institutional discrimination therefore influenced not only who occupied positions of authority, but also which athletes were most exposed to long-term physical consequence. Understanding how football organizations distributed athletes across positions is therefore essential for examining how racial inequality became tied directly to bodily harm within the modern NFL.

Section 3

Bodily Risk and Injury Burden

The institutional systems shaping positional assignment within football carried consequences extending far beyond representation alone. By disproportionately concentrating Black athletes within positions associated with speed, collision, and bodily exposure, racial stacking also influenced how physical risk and long-term injury burden became distributed throughout the sport. As a result, the historical separation between “thinking” positions and “physical” positions did not simply shape leadership opportunities—it also shaped which athletes were most exposed to bodily harm.

Football has long relied upon a labor structure in which certain positions absorb significantly greater physical punishment than others. Running backs, wide receivers, linebackers, defensive linemen, and defensive backs experience repeated high-speed collisions, open-field tackles, trench contact, and physically demanding workloads throughout both games and practices. Quarterbacks, by contrast, often receive greater institutional protection through rules, offensive schemes, and positional value. Because Black athletes became disproportionately concentrated within many of these physically intensive positions, the unequal distribution of bodily risk became deeply connected to racialized positional systems.

This relationship between race and bodily consequence reflects broader historical patterns discussed throughout earlier sections of this project. As sociologists studying race and sport have argued, Black athletes were historically valued within football institutions primarily for bodily performance and physical labor rather than leadership or organizational authority (Coakley, 2012). Drew Brown’s Race, Colonization and the NFL Draft: A Fanonian Analysis of the Interviewing of Black NFL Prospects further explains that scouting and recruitment systems frequently framed Black athletes through assumptions emphasizing physical explosiveness, athletic upside, and bodily performance rather than strategic leadership or intellectual control (Brown, 2023). These institutional patterns helped funnel Black athletes toward positions carrying elevated levels of physical exposure and long-term bodily sacrifice.

Importantly, the unequal distribution of bodily risk within football also shaped how athletes were valued economically and institutionally. In Tackling Bias: The Racial Lens on NFL Injury Compensation, the author argues that racial disparities influenced not only positional assignment, but also how injury and long-term physical damage were interpreted and compensated within football organizations (“Tackling Bias,” 2024). The article explains that Black athletes were often expected to endure greater levels of physical sacrifice while simultaneously facing structural disadvantages within compensation systems related to injury and long-term neurological harm. These disparities demonstrate that racial inequality within football extended beyond access to leadership positions and became tied directly to bodily consequence.

The visualization above demonstrates the relationship between racial concentration and injury burden across major NFL positional groups. To improve positional accuracy, the visualization combines the original injury dataset with detailed positional roster data from nflverse, allowing broader defensive categories to be separated into defensive back (DB), linebacker (LB), and defensive line (DL) groupings rather than treating all defensive players as a single category. The merged dataset draws from NFL player and injury records spanning multiple seasons between 2009 and 2017 and compares average injury burden alongside the percentage of Black players occupying each position group. Bubble size represents the cumulative number of games missed due to injury within each positional category, while the x-axis measures average injury burden and the y-axis measures Black player representation.

Several important patterns emerge from the visualization. Running back remains the clearest example of racialized bodily exposure within the modern NFL. The position combines the highest injury burden in the dataset with the highest percentage of Black player representation, reinforcing the project’s broader argument that the positions most associated with Black athletes often carry elevated physical consequences. Wide receiver, defensive back, defensive line, and linebacker positions also show high Black representation, though their injury burden varies by role. This variation is important because it shows that racialized bodily exposure is not evenly distributed across every Black-majority position; rather, it depends on the specific physical demands of each positional role.

At the same time, the visualization complicates simplistic assumptions regarding bodily risk by revealing the unique position occupied by offensive linemen. Offensive line positions contain substantially lower Black representation than many other physically intensive positions—approximately 36.3% within this dataset—yet they produce one of the largest bubbles in the visualization, indicating a high cumulative number of games missed due to injury. This suggests that bodily exposure within football is not distributed solely through race, but also through the structural demands associated with particular forms of labor within the sport. Offensive linemen experience constant physical strain through repetitive contact, joint stress, and trench collisions despite occupying a positional category that is not majority Black.

The separation of defensive positions into DB, LB, and DL categories also reveals important internal variation within defensive labor itself. Defensive backs and defensive linemen maintain high Black representation, while linebackers occupy a somewhat more moderate position between defensive and offensive extremes. By disaggregating defense into more precise positional groups through the nflverse roster merge, the visualization avoids overstating defensive labor as a singular category and instead demonstrates how bodily exposure varies across different forms of defensive responsibility. This methodological refinement strengthens the broader argument that racial stacking operates not simply across offense versus defense, but across highly specific forms of positional labor.

The distribution shown above reflects more than coincidence. The positions most heavily associated with Black athletes remain among the most physically punishing positions within football itself. Running backs, defensive backs, defensive linemen, linebackers, and wide receivers experience bodily exposure through repeated collisions, tackling responsibilities, acceleration demands, and open-field contact. These positional demands increase the likelihood of missed games, accumulated physical wear, and long-term injury risk. Consequently, racial stacking did not simply determine who occupied leadership positions; it also influenced which athletes disproportionately absorbed the physical consequences of the sport.

Importantly, these inequalities were not accidental byproducts of athletic specialization alone. Rather, they emerged through decades of institutional decision-making, recruitment systems, scouting practices, and organizational assumptions surrounding race and labor. Football organizations consistently directed Black athletes toward positions emphasizing physical production while preserving positions associated with leadership, authority, and institutional control for white athletes. The unequal distribution of injury burden therefore reflects the material consequences of racial stacking itself.

At the same time, football culture frequently normalized these disparities through media narratives that framed Black athletes as naturally suited for physically demanding positions. Descriptions emphasizing aggression, explosiveness, toughness, and “natural athleticism” helped reinforce the belief that bodily sacrifice was an expected or inherent aspect of Black athletic participation. Understanding how these narratives circulated throughout football media and public discourse is therefore essential for examining how racial inequality became culturally reinforced within the modern NFL.

Section 4

Media Narratives and Cultural Reinforcement

Although racial stacking shaped the structural organization of football, media discourse and scouting language helped normalize and reinforce those divisions culturally. Long after Black athletes began entering positions previously dominated by white players, commentators, scouts, journalists, and executives continued describing players through racialized assumptions about intelligence, athleticism, leadership, and emotional control. These narratives did not simply reflect existing stereotypes; they actively reproduced them for audiences, organizations, and future generations of players. Through television commentary, scouting reports, draft analysis, and sports journalism, football culture repeatedly framed white athletes as intellectual leaders while presenting Black athletes as naturally physical, instinctive, or athletically gifted.

The persistence of these narratives is especially visible in discussions surrounding the quarterback position. For decades, quarterback was treated as the symbolic center of intelligence and leadership in football culture. As discussed earlier, Black quarterbacks historically faced skepticism because owners, coaches, and media commentators associated the position with rationality, discipline, and decision-making—traits culturally coded as white. The Guardian’s analysis of Black quarterbacks explains that many critics continued to describe Black quarterbacks as “athletes first” even when they demonstrated elite passing ability and strategic intelligence (Powell, 2019). Rather than evaluating quarterbacks equally, media narratives often separated white quarterbacks into “thinking” categories and Black quarterbacks into “physical” categories.

This pattern extended beyond commentary and into the formal language used during scouting and draft evaluation processes. Lund and Katballe-Kristensen’s leadership stereotype study demonstrates that online scouting profiles systematically framed quarterbacks differently depending on race. White quarterbacks were significantly more likely to receive descriptors associated with intelligence, leadership, composure, and personality, while African American quarterbacks were more frequently associated with athleticism and raw physical talent. The study found that white quarterbacks averaged 1.68 intelligence-related concepts per scouting profile compared to only 0.95 for Black quarterbacks, while Black quarterbacks averaged 1.77 athleticism-related concepts compared to only 0.34 for white quarterbacks (Lund & Katballe-Kristensen, 2025). These differences reveal how racial stereotypes became embedded within supposedly objective systems of player evaluation.

The visualization above illustrates how racialized scouting language differed between white and African American quarterbacks in online NFL draft profiles. Using data from Lund and Katballe-Kristensen’s analysis of 132 scouting reports covering 66 quarterbacks, the chart compares the average number of coded descriptor concepts assigned to each racial group across intelligence, athleticism, and personality categories. White quarterbacks received substantially more intelligence and personality-oriented descriptors, while Black quarterbacks received dramatically more athleticism-oriented language. The visualization demonstrates how evaluative language itself reinforced racial assumptions about leadership and physicality. Even before entering the NFL, quarterbacks were framed through narratives that associated whiteness with intelligence and Blackness with athletic instinct.

These patterns connect directly to the broader sociological concept of “natural athleticism” narratives. The sociology review article argues that Black athletes have historically been portrayed as naturally gifted bodies rather than disciplined intellectual competitors. Such framing minimizes preparation, strategic understanding, and leadership while emphasizing instinct and physical superiority. White athletes, by contrast, are more frequently described through narratives emphasizing work ethic, intelligence, preparation, and mental discipline. These distinctions may appear subtle, but repeated exposure to such language shapes audience perception over time. Media consumers begin unconsciously associating Black athletes with bodily performance while associating white athletes with intelligence, authority, and leadership.

Drew Brown’s Race, Colonization and the NFL Draft extends this argument by explaining how scouting and evaluative systems reproduce racial hierarchy through coded language. Brown argues that NFL evaluation processes frequently positioned Black prospects within stereotypes connected to aggression, violence, athleticism, and emotionality while framing white prospects as disciplined decision-makers capable of leadership and authority (Brown, 2010). Importantly, these narratives were often presented as neutral football analysis rather than overt racism. Because the stereotypes operated through subtle evaluative language rather than explicit exclusion, they became easier to normalize within football culture.

The influence of these narratives extends beyond media representation alone. Scouting reports, commentary, and draft analysis shape public expectations, organizational decision-making, and even player self-perception. When Black quarterbacks are consistently described as athletic rather than intelligent, organizations become more likely to question their leadership ability, audiences become more skeptical of their decision-making, and coaching staffs may unconsciously evaluate them differently. Media discourse therefore functions as a cultural reinforcement mechanism that helps sustain institutional inequality within football.

These narratives also help justify the unequal structures examined throughout this project. The same stereotypes that framed Black athletes as naturally physical contributed to their concentration within higher-risk positions, their exclusion from leadership roles, and the normalization of bodily sacrifice within the sport. By repeatedly associating Black athletes with physicality rather than intellect, football culture reinforced the broader racial hierarchy that shaped positional segregation, institutional authority, and bodily risk across the modern NFL.

Section 5

Reform, Resistance, and the Present

Although racial stacking and institutional inequality remain deeply embedded within football culture, resistance to these structures has steadily expanded throughout the modern era of the NFL. Players, coaches, journalists, scholars, and activists have increasingly challenged the racial assumptions that historically shaped positional assignment, organizational power, and bodily labor within the sport. The contemporary NFL exists within a period of contradiction: while major breakthroughs and reforms have occurred, many of the structural inequalities explored throughout this project continue to persist in subtler forms.

One of the clearest signs of change has been the increasing visibility and success of Black quarterbacks in the modern NFL. For decades, Black athletes were systematically excluded from quarterback because owners, coaches, and commentators associated the position with discipline, composure, and decision-making traits culturally coded as white. However, the success of players such as Doug Williams, Warren Moon, Randall Cunningham, Russell Wilson, Lamar Jackson, Patrick Mahomes, and Jalen Hurts challenged the assumption that quarterback leadership was inherently tied to whiteness. Their success demonstrated that the historical exclusion of Black quarterbacks reflected institutional prejudice rather than athletic reality.

At the organizational level, the NFL also faced growing pressure to address racial inequality in hiring practices. The implementation of the Rooney Rule represented one of the league’s most public attempts to reform institutional discrimination by requiring teams to interview minority candidates for head coaching positions. While the policy created additional opportunities for some candidates, scholars have argued that it often functioned more as a symbolic reform than a complete structural transformation. Cunningham and Melton (2011) explain that informal ownership networks, organizational culture, and unconscious assumptions about leadership continued shaping hiring decisions even after formal diversity policies were introduced. As a result, Black coaches and executives remained underrepresented relative to the overwhelmingly Black player population.

Modern players and activists have also increasingly challenged the broader culture surrounding football itself. Public conversations surrounding concussion trauma, player safety, racial inequality, and labor exploitation have expanded dramatically during the past decade. Athletes have used interviews, documentaries, social media, and public protest to criticize how the league profits from bodily sacrifice while limiting player authority and long-term protection. Discussions surrounding Colin Kaepernick, player activism, racial justice protests, and collective bargaining disputes demonstrate how football has become a broader site of political and cultural conflict extending far beyond the field itself.

The rise of publicly available sports analytics and data has also created new opportunities to challenge traditional narratives. Much of this project relied on public roster, injury, demographic, and scouting datasets to visualize relationships that historically remained hidden beneath anecdotal discussion. By combining positional demographics, injury burden data, scouting language analysis, and organizational trends, these visualizations expose how racial inequality can persist through systems that often appear neutral or meritocratic on the surface. Data visualization therefore becomes not only an analytical tool, but also a method of revealing structural relationships between race, labor, organizational power, and bodily risk within professional football.

Importantly, the persistence of racial stacking cannot be separated from the broader economic realities facing many Black athletes. As several of the sources discussed throughout this project argue, football has historically been presented as one of the few accessible paths toward wealth, social mobility, and public recognition for young Black men in the United States. This promise of upward mobility helped funnel Black athletes into positions associated with extreme bodily exposure and physical sacrifice. Positions such as running back, defensive back, linebacker, and defensive line became both opportunities for economic advancement and sites of disproportionate bodily risk. The league’s structure therefore reflects a deeper contradiction: Black athletes were often encouraged to pursue success through positions demanding the greatest physical punishment while remaining systematically underrepresented within positions of long-term organizational power.

These tensions ultimately raise broader questions about meritocracy, institutional power, and the cultural meaning of leadership within American sports. Although reforms and resistance movements have challenged many historical barriers, the modern NFL continues reflecting broader social systems connecting race, labor, bodily risk, and institutional inequality.

Conclusion

Football as a Structure of Race, Labor, and Bodily Value

The history of racial stacking in American football reveals how sports can reproduce broader systems of racial inequality while simultaneously presenting themselves as meritocratic spaces. Throughout the development of the modern NFL, racial stereotypes shaped which athletes were trusted with strategic responsibility and which athletes were directed toward positions emphasizing bodily labor, collision, and physical sacrifice. Black athletes became disproportionately concentrated within positions associated with speed, aggression, and physical punishment, while positions tied to organizational control and symbolic leadership remained disproportionately white for much of football’s history.

The visualizations and datasets analyzed throughout this project demonstrate how these historical structures continue influencing the modern NFL. Positional demographics reveal that Black athletes remain heavily concentrated within positions associated with heightened physical exposure, while leadership positions within coaching, executive management, and ownership remain disproportionately white. Injury burden analysis further demonstrates that many positions most associated with Black athletes frequently carry elevated levels of bodily risk and physical consequence. At the same time, scouting language and media discourse continue reproducing subtle distinctions between “intelligent” leadership and “natural” athleticism that mirror older racial stereotypes surrounding football labor.

Importantly, these inequalities cannot be separated from the broader economic realities surrounding football itself. For many Black athletes, football has historically represented one of the few visible paths toward wealth, social mobility, education, and public recognition within the United States. The promise of upward mobility encouraged generations of Black athletes to pursue success within positions demanding extraordinary physical sacrifice and long-term bodily risk. As a result, football became both a source of opportunity and a system of exploitation.

At the same time, resistance to these structures has continued expanding throughout the modern era of football. The rise of Black quarterbacks, player activism, minority hiring initiatives, and increasing public discussions surrounding race and bodily risk demonstrate that many athletes, scholars, journalists, and activists continue challenging the assumptions historically embedded within the sport. By combining historical research with modern data visualization, this project demonstrates how computational analysis can strengthen critical examinations of race and institutional power within sports culture.

Ultimately, racial stacking in football reflects larger tensions within American society itself. Football became a cultural space where assumptions about race, labor, leadership, and bodily value were organized into visible hierarchies and reproduced across generations. Although important progress has occurred, the persistence of unequal representation, bodily risk, and institutional authority demonstrates that these systems have not fully disappeared.
